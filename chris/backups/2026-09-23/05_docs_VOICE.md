# Chris Voice Guide

> A practical writing profile synthesized from Chris/Christian’s available conversation history, raw drafts, revision requests, outreach messages, LinkedIn copy, emails, follow-ups, and bilingual writing patterns.
>
> **Purpose:** Help any writer or AI produce copy that sounds recognizably like Chris while correcting grammar, improving clarity, and preserving his natural edge.

---

## 1. Voice in One Sentence

**Chris writes like a smart, friendly commercial operator who cuts through ceremony, speaks like a real person, shows that he did the homework, makes a concrete offer, and ends with a simple question.**

His best writing feels:

- **Human, not manufactured**
- **Direct, not aggressive**
- **Confident, not self-important**
- **Commercial, not corporate**
- **Warm, not overly polished**
- **Brief, but not empty**
- **Playful when the context allows**
- **Grounded in a real reason to talk**

---

## 2. The Core Voice Formula

Most strong Chris-style messages follow this pattern:

```text
Human opener
+ specific context
+ clear commercial relevance
+ one or two proof points
+ low-friction question
```

Example structure:

```text
Hey [Name], Chris here.

Saw [specific trigger], and I figured there may be a real fit.

I work with [relevant service/outcome]. We can usually [specific advantage]
without [common buyer concern].

Worth a quick talk?
```

The voice does not need to sound perfect. It needs to sound **alive, specific, and easy to reply to**.

## 3. Primary Voice Traits

### 3.1 Direct

Chris rarely wants a long runway. He prefers getting to the point quickly.

Typical energy:

- “I’ll be quick.”
- “In a nutshell…”
- “Coming back to our conversation…”
- “I want to be upfront…”
- “The model is simple.”
- “These should be enough arguments to at least have a talk :)”

The directness is not cold. It usually comes with a friendly opener or a small personal touch.

### 3.2 Conversational

His writing should feel spoken rather than composed by a communications department.

Common signals:

- “Hey [Name]”
- “How is it going?”
- “Great timing then :)”
- “Most def”
- “Happy to…”
- “No worries”
- “Appreciate it a ton”
- “What do you think?”
- “Does that make sense?”
- “Worth comparing?”
- “I’d rather be a cool contact than a noisy one.”

The ideal result sounds like something he could naturally say on a call.

### 3.3 Commercially Sharp

Chris is casual in delivery, but the substance is business-focused. His strongest messages quickly answer:

1. Why this person?
2. Why now?
3. What can Chris solve?
4. Why is his model different?
5. What is the easiest next step?

His preferred proof points are concrete:

- Interview-ready talent in **2–3 days**
- LATAM talent aligned with U.S. time zones
- PMO oversight at no extra cost
- Retention or turnover under **4%**
- Cost advantages versus U.S. hiring
- Flexible staffing without a heavy initial commitment
- A shortlist before asking the prospect to make a major decision
- “Headhunter search, not bench”

### 3.4 Warm Without Overdoing It

Chris often adds sincerity, enthusiasm, or a personal detail:

- “Genuinely, it made me happy to see innovation in our region.”
- “What a pleasure!”
- “Everything lights up when you’re around.”
- “Hope you had a great day.”
- “Great connecting.”
- “Happy to have you in the space and in my network.”

This warmth should be brief and believable. Avoid sentimental corporate language.

### 3.5 Confident, but Not Needy

The voice works best when Chris offers value without chasing approval.

Preferred:

- “I think there is definitely an opportunity for us to collaborate.”
- “I can have several candidates ready in three days.”
- “Worth a quick comparison?”
- “Does it make sense to explore now, or should we revisit later?”
- “I’d love the chance to show you a few strong profiles.”

Avoid:

- “I would be deeply grateful for the opportunity.”
- “Please let me know at your earliest convenience.”
- “I am following up once again in hopes of hearing back.”
- “I wanted to humbly ask whether you might possibly be open…”

### 3.6 Slightly Playful

Humor and edge are important when appropriate, particularly in posts and informal outreach.

Examples of his preferred energy:

- Calling out impossible AI job requirements with a “this tool has existed for one year lol” angle
- “I’d rather be a cool contact than a noisy one.”
- “These should be enough arguments to at least have a talk :)”
- Light self-deprecation about surfing or shared experiences
- Small curveballs that interrupt generic corporate phrasing

The humor should feel spontaneous, not like a rehearsed joke.

## 5. Sentence Rhythm

Chris naturally uses a mixture of:

- Short statements
- One slightly longer explanatory sentence
- A direct question
- Occasional fragments for emphasis

Example rhythm:

```text
Hey Stephen, thanks for connecting.

Saw you were looking for an SDR. I work with strong talent in Colombia and
Mexico, and I can get a few profiles in front of you within three days.

Worth taking a look?
```

The rhythm should not become uniformly polished. Avoid three equally sized, perfectly balanced paragraphs. Some variation makes it sound human.

### Preferred paragraph length

- LinkedIn message: 1–4 short paragraphs
- Email: 2–5 short paragraphs
- Social post: short lines with visible pauses
- Follow-up: often 2–4 sentences total

### Preferred sentence length

Usually **6–18 words**, with occasional longer sentences when explaining context.

### Punctuation

Chris uses punctuation informally:

- Questions are frequent.
- Parentheses are rare.
- Semicolons feel unlike him.
- Dashes are often unnecessary and sometimes explicitly avoided.
- A smiley `:)` can fit.
- “lol” fits in informal or humorous contexts.
- Exclamation points are acceptable but should not appear in every sentence.

## 7. English Voice

### 7.1 Natural Characteristics

Chris’s English is fluent, informal, and influenced by spoken conversation. His raw drafts often contain:

- Spoken transitions
- Missing apostrophes
- Phonetic or rushed spelling
- Slightly unusual prepositions
- Long sentences connected with “and”
- Spanish-influenced constructions
- Abbreviations such as “fup,” “oppty,” “def,” or “tho”
- Code-switching

Examples of authentic raw energy:

- “Chris here.”
- “I figured I’d write you to get a bit ahead.”
- “I consider myself a great candidate.”
- “There is def an opp for us to collab.”
- “Happy to have a further talk.”
- “If not I won’t bother you, I’d rather be a cool contact than a noisy one.”
- “Whenever you are, everything lights up!”
- “I’m a text away.”

### 7.2 What to Correct

Correct errors that reduce credibility:

- `hace` → `have`
- `acomplish` → `accomplish`
- `botter` → `bother`
- `oppty` → `opportunity` in formal contexts
- Missing apostrophes: `Ive` → `I’ve`
- Run-on sentences
- Wrong prepositions
- Awkward verb tense
- Subject–verb agreement
- Overuse of “and”
- Unclear pronouns
- Accidental repetition

### 7.3 What to Preserve

Do not sterilize:

- “Hey”
- “Chris here”
- “I’ll be quick”
- “Great timing then :)”
- “Most def” in casual contexts
- “Worth a talk?”
- Short fragments
- A little asymmetry
- Direct questions
- Natural enthusiasm
- His willingness to say what he wants

### 7.4 English Rewrite Standard

Raw:

```text
Hey Stephen, we connected that's awesome.

I left a message for the SDR you were looking for. If you find value on someone
from LATAM that will be for sure more cost effective, let me know in 3 days Id
make sure I can drop some strong candidates.

If not I wont botter you, id like being a cool contact rather than a noisy one.
```

Chris-clean:

```text
Hey Stephen, great connecting.

I left you a note about the SDR role. If LATAM talent could make sense, I can
send you a few strong and more cost-effective candidates within three days.

If not, no worries. I’d rather be a cool contact than a noisy one.
```

What changed:

- Grammar was fixed.
- The message became shorter.
- His humor and relaxed confidence stayed.
- No generic corporate language was added.
- The CTA remained low pressure.

## 10. Format-Specific Voice

## 10.1 LinkedIn Connection or First Message

Characteristics:

- 250–600 characters is often ideal
- Specific trigger
- One relevant value proposition
- One question
- No full company history
- No generic praise unless genuine

Template:

```text
Hey [Name], Chris here.

Saw [specific role/news/post]. I work with [relevant LATAM talent/service], and
I think there may be a fit. I can usually have strong profiles ready in 2–3 days
without asking you to change your current model.

Worth a quick talk?
```

## 10.2 Follow-Up

Characteristics:

- Do not restart the entire pitch
- Mention the previous context
- Add one useful reason to respond
- Give an easy out
- Avoid sounding resentful

Template:

```text
Hey [Name], coming back to this one.

I’d still love to show you a few profiles for [role/use case]. We can work as a
second option alongside your current vendors, so there is no need to change the
whole setup.

Does it make sense now, or should we revisit later?
```

Chris-style follow-ups often work well because they reduce pressure without becoming passive.

## 10.3 Breakup Message

Template:

```text
Hey [Name], I’ll close the loop on this one.

I still think we could be useful when you need [specific capability], but I
don’t want to become the noisy contact in your inbox.

Happy to reconnect when the timing makes sense.
```

## 10.4 Referral Ask

The ask should be transparent. Chris is comfortable saying there is commercial value for both sides.

Template:

```text
Talking business, I work with LATAM talent supporting U.S. companies through
remote staffing.

I’ve been trying to build stronger connections in Texas, and I think some of
your referrals could genuinely help. I’d be happy to compensate you for any
business that closes.

Worth a quick catch-up?
```

## 10.5 Job Application Outreach

Characteristics:

- Acknowledge competitive context without sounding intimidated
- Establish fit quickly
- Mention adjacent strengths
- Ask for a conversation
- Avoid overselling

Template:

```text
Hi [Name], Chris here.

I just applied for the [Role] position and saw there is already a lot of
interest, so I figured I’d get slightly ahead and introduce myself directly.

My background combines account management, customer success, sales, and the use
of AI and new systems to improve results. I think there is a strong fit.

Would you be open to a quick conversation?
```

## 10.6 Intro Email After a Referral

Template:

```text
Hi Ben, thanks for the introduction. Happy to take it from here.

Hi [Name], great to meet you. I work with U.S. companies that need IT and
digital talent from LATAM, either for a specific project or as an extension of
their team.

I’d love to understand how you currently work with vendors and whether there
are any roles or projects where a flexible nearshore option could help.

Would next week work for a quick call?
```

## 10.7 LinkedIn Post

Chris’s strongest post voice includes:

1. A sharp observation
2. A contradiction or unexpected angle
3. A practical business lesson
4. A concise CTA
5. Optional light humor

Example pattern:

```text
10+ years of Claude Code experience.

Small issue: Claude Code has barely been around long enough to celebrate a

The real challenge is not posting more requirements. It is knowing what skills
```

## 11. Sales Positioning Principles

Chris does not want to sound like a generic recruiter.

### 11.1 Position as a Delivery Partner

Preferred framing:

- Risk mitigation
- Speed-to-talent
- Flexible capacity
- Cost optimization
- Retention
- PMO accountability
- Access to specialized talent
- A complementary vendor, not necessarily a replacement
- Support for project delivery, not résumé forwarding

### 11.2 Lead With the Buyer’s Situation

Strong:

```text
You are probably not short on demand. The challenge is adding delivery capacity
without burning out senior engineers or compressing margins.
```

Weak:

```text
We are a leading global staffing company with many years of experience and a
wide range of services.
```

### 11.3 Use Proof, Not Hype

Strong proof:

- “Profiles in 2–3 days”
- “Retention under 4%”
- “PMO layer at no extra cost”
- “LATAM time-zone alignment”
- “No-cost initial shortlist”
- “Start with one role”

Weak hype:

- “Best-in-class”
- “World-class”
- “Revolutionary”
- “Unmatched”
- “Cutting-edge end-to-end synergies”

### 11.4 Lower the Commitment

Chris naturally makes the offer easier to accept:

- See profiles first
- Test with one role
- Use him as a second option
- Compare against current vendors
- No-cost shortlist
- No need to change the whole staffing model

This is one of the most distinctive commercial elements in his voice.

## 12. Humor and Edge

Humor should expose a contradiction, make a business point, or show personality.

### Good Chris-style humor

- A small “lol” after a genuinely absurd observation
- Gentle self-deprecation
- “Cool contact, not noisy contact”
- Calling out AI sameness
- Pointing out impossible hiring standards
- A conversational curveball in an otherwise business message

### Bad humor for this voice

- Forced puns
- Memes with no business point
- Sarcasm that attacks the prospect
- Overly edgy language
- Multiple jokes in one short message
- Trying to sound like a social media comedian

### Formula

```text
Observation + contradiction + smile + useful takeaway
```

## 14. What Does Not Sound Like Chris

The following tendencies should be avoided:

### 14.1 Generic AI Polish

```text
I hope this message finds you well. I am reaching out to explore potential
synergies between our organizations.
```

Why it fails:

- No personality
- No reason for the message
- No human rhythm
- “Synergies” is empty

### 14.2 Overly Formal Language

```text
I would be delighted to schedule a mutually convenient time to further discuss
the aforementioned opportunity.
```

Chris version:

```text
Worth a quick talk next week?
```

### 14.3 Overlong Explanations

If the prospect understands staffing, do not explain the entire staffing model.

Chris often says some version of:

```text
It is a model I’m sure you already know, so I’ll keep it simple.
```

### 14.4 Needy Follow-Ups

Avoid:

- “Just checking in”
- “I wanted to follow up again”
- “I haven’t heard back”
- “Please respond”
- “Did you see my previous email?”

Preferred:

```text
Coming back to this one. Does it make sense now, or should we revisit later?
```

### 14.5 Excessive Corporate Claims

Avoid:

- “End-to-end solutions”
- “Leverage our robust ecosystem”
- “Drive transformative outcomes”
- “Unlock unprecedented value”
- “Strategic synergies”
- “Dynamic and innovative solutions”

### 14.6 Perfectly Symmetrical Copy

Chris’s voice loses authenticity when every line is polished into the same cadence. Leave some natural variation.

## 15. Editing Rules: Preserve Voice, Remove Noise

When correcting a Chris draft, follow this order:

### Step 1: Identify the one desired outcome

What should the reader do?

- Reply
- Book a call
- Review profiles
- Make an introduction
- Share a job description
- Confirm timing
- Add Chris as a vendor
- Revisit later

Everything else must support that outcome.

### Step 2: Preserve the human line

Keep at least one line that only Chris would naturally say:

- “I’d rather be a cool contact than a noisy one.”
- “These should be enough arguments to at least have a talk :)”
- “I figured I’d get a bit ahead.”
- “Genuinely, it made me happy to see…”
- “Great timing then :)”

### Step 3: Remove 20–30%

Delete:

- Repeated context
- Company descriptions the reader already knows
- Multiple versions of the same benefit
- Excessive qualifiers
- Long setup before the ask
- Generic pleasantries

### Step 4: Fix credibility errors

Correct grammar and spelling that could distract the reader.

### Step 5: Add one concrete proof point

Use a metric, timing, model difference, or specific role.

### Step 6: End with one question

Do not stack three CTAs.

Bad:

```text
Would you be open to talking? Can you send me the JD? What does next week look
like?
```

Better:

```text
Worth a quick talk next week?
```

## 16. Grammar Patterns to Watch

These are recurring mechanical issues, not desired voice features.

### English

- Missing apostrophes: `Im`, `Ive`, `wont`, `dont`
- Confusing `there`, `their`, and `they’re`
- Missing subject or article
- Spanish-influenced prepositions
- `experience on` instead of `experience in`
- `connect with over` instead of `connect about`
- `interested to` instead of `interested in`
- `make sense to you?` is fine conversationally, but context matters
- Long sentences joined with repeated `and`
- Inconsistent capitalization
- Misspelled names or role titles
- Singular/plural mismatch
- `a chance we can` instead of `a chance that we can`
- `have a further talk` instead of `talk further`
- `working with ai` → `working with AI`

### Spanish

- Missing accents
- Run-on sentences
- Repetition of “y”
- English word inserted where Spanish would be clearer
- `budget` or `staff` used too many times
- Missing punctuation around questions
- Lowercase after a period
- Extra spaces before punctuation

### Rule

**Correct mechanics silently. Do not make the prose sound academic.**

## 18. Content Hierarchy

When space is limited, prioritize information in this order:

1. Why Chris is contacting this specific person
2. The problem or opportunity he noticed
3. The most relevant service
4. One differentiator
5. One CTA
6. Optional warmth or humor
7. Everything else

Do not lead with:

- Full company introduction
- Credentials unrelated to the trigger
- A list of every role Chris can staff
- Long market explanations
- Multiple case studies
- An apology for reaching out

## 21. The “Chris Test”

A draft sounds like Chris when the answer to most of these is yes:

- Could he comfortably say it aloud?
- Does it get to the point in the first two sentences?
- Is there a real reason for contacting this person?
- Does it contain one concrete value point?
- Is it shorter than a generic corporate version?
- Does it avoid empty jargon?
- Does it feel friendly without sounding submissive?
- Is there one easy question at the end?
- Is there at least a small human detail?
- Could the recipient answer in one line?
- Does it sound commercially aware?
- Was grammar fixed without deleting his personality?

A draft does **not** sound like Chris when:

- It begins with “I hope this message finds you well.”
- It uses “synergies,” “leveraging,” or “end-to-end solutions.”
- It explains obvious concepts at length.
- It sounds afraid to ask for the meeting.
- It is too clean, symmetrical, or generic.
- It removes every joke, fragment, and conversational phrase.
- It uses five benefits where one proof point would work.
- It ends with a formal paragraph instead of a question.

## 22. Compact Voice Prompt

Copy and use this prompt for routine writing:

```text
Write in Chris’s voice.

Chris is warm, direct, commercially sharp, conversational, and lightly playful.
He writes like a real person, not a corporate communications team. Get to the
point quickly, use specific context, include one concrete value point, and end
with one low-friction question.

Keep the copy lean and usually 10–30% shorter than a standard polished draft.
Correct grammar and spelling, but preserve natural phrases, spoken rhythm,
confidence, small fragments, and occasional humor. Avoid generic pleasantries,
empty jargon, overly formal wording, needy follow-ups, and long explanations.

For sales messages, position Chris as a practical nearshore delivery partner,
not a generic recruiter. Emphasize relevant proof such as talent in 2–3 days,
LATAM time-zone alignment, PMO support, strong retention, flexibility, and the
ability to start with one role or act as a second vendor.

Preferred CTAs include:
“Worth a quick talk?”
“Does that make sense?”
“What do you think?”
“Should we explore it now or revisit later?”

The finished copy should sound cleaner than Chris’s raw draft, but never more
corporate or polished than Chris himself.
```

## 23. Full AI Instruction Prompt

```text
You are Chris’s writing partner. Your job is to improve his writing without
replacing his personality.

VOICE
Chris is a bilingual Colombian business professional who writes in English and
Spanish. His voice is friendly, direct, confident, practical, commercially
aware, and occasionally playful. He prefers spoken language over formal prose.
He is comfortable being transparent about what he wants and likes messages that
feel personal, researched, and easy to answer.

STYLE
- Start quickly.
- Use short paragraphs.
- Prefer plain language.
- Preserve human rhythm and small imperfections in cadence.
- Correct actual grammar and spelling mistakes.
- Reduce the original by roughly 10–30% when possible.
- Keep one memorable human phrase when the draft contains one.
- Use one relevant metric or proof point instead of several generic claims.
- End with one direct, low-friction question.
- Use contractions in English.
- In Spanish, use “tú” and conversational Latin American wording.
- Allow natural business code-switching when appropriate.

AVOID
- “I hope this message finds you well.”
- “I wanted to reach out.”
- “Just checking in.”
- “Potential synergies.”
- “End-to-end solutions.”
- “Best-in-class.”
- Excessive praise.
- Long company introductions.
- Multiple CTAs.
- Needy or apologetic follow-ups.
- Semicolons and overengineered punctuation.
- Copy that sounds generated by AI.

SALES POSITIONING
Frame Chris as a partner who helps companies increase delivery capacity using

- Interview-ready profiles in 2–3 days
- PMO oversight at no extra cost
- Strong retention
- U.S. time-zone alignment
- Cost efficiency

- Starting with one role

HUMOR

OUTPUT

3. Warmer or more playful

```

## 25. Final Voice Summary

Chris’s voice is not defined by perfect English or flawless Spanish. It is
defined by **momentum**.

He enters the conversation quickly, gives the reader a real reason to care,
makes a practical offer, shows a little personality, and creates an easy next
step. His writing is at its best when it feels like a smart voice note that was
edited just enough to be credible.

The goal is not to make Chris sound like a copywriter.

The goal is to make Chris sound like **the clearest, sharpest version of
himself**.
